Dear Editor,

We are pleased to submit our manuscript, “Everyday rain, not just storms: Rainfall and intercity travel in China”, for consideration as a research paper in *Transportation Research Part D*.

Climate adaptation of transport systems has focused on extreme events, typically one storm or flood at a time. Your journal recently published evidence on how a single heavy-rain event disrupted cross-city mobility in Japan (Liu et al., 2026, *TR-D* 158, 105446). Our paper asks a complementary question at national scale: how does the full range of daily rainfall, from light rain to typhoon downpours and winter snow, affect travel between cities?

We combine 28.8 million daily origin–destination observations on 26,442 stable links among 365 Chinese cities (2023–2025) with reanalysis rainfall at weather-station locations, and identify effects from day-to-day weather variation within links. The main findings are:

- On ordinary days, intercity flows fall smoothly with rainfall, by about 2% in moderate rain and 4–6% in rainstorms, with a further fall the next day; light rain matters only at the destination.
- Effects decay with distance. Trips under 150 km respond most, links under 300 km bear 86% of the loss, and on these links weekend trips respond 40–50% more to heavy rain than workday trips.
- Cities in drier climates respond more strongly to moderate and heavier rain, consistent with adaptation.
- Snow-prone days and typhoon days are distinct: the same precipitation disrupts travel several times more. Separating them reveals three channels: a demand response to everyday rain, hazardous roads on snow-prone days and supply disruption on typhoon days.
- Because modest rain is so common, it removes an estimated 0.9–1.1 billion intercity trips a year once next-day effects are counted, far more than rare extremes.

The main results hold with temperature controls, gauge-based rainfall, region-by-date fixed effects, placebo tests, finer fixed effects, Poisson estimation and three-way clustered inference. They imply that adapting regional transport to a changing climate should include routine improvements on short intercity corridors, not only emergency responses to extreme events, which we believe makes the paper of interest to the journal's readers in transport planning and climate adaptation.
